Our thesis is that on the sites we build on, a building's height is not a design decision.
It is arithmetic, and it runs backward. Start from the number of parking stalls the building
is obliged to provide for every thousand square feet of space it rents out. Add the furthest
anyone may reasonably be asked to walk from a stall to the front door. Measure the parcel.
The number of floors the building can be falls out of those three facts, and it falls out
before an architect has drawn anything.

This runs against the ordinary instinct, which is that a building is as tall as the zoning
allows and the market will absorb. On a site with a rail station under it, that instinct is
roughly right. On the sites we buy — the large-format retail nodes in smaller regional
markets, where there is no rapid transit and, by our own site-selection criteria, is not
going to be — it is wrong, because the binding constraint is not the height limit in the
zoning bylaw. It is the land the cars need, and the distance from the cars to the door.

This paper is about that geometry and nothing else. It is deliberately not about how a
building gets financed, which is a genuinely different question with its own answer
elsewhere: a companion paper on our investment-thesis site sets out the financing sequence.
The two are not competing answers to the same question, and it is worth saying so plainly
because at a glance they can look like it. Geometry sets the ceiling — the tallest building
this parcel can physically support given the cars. Financing determines whether, and how
well, a project actually reaches that ceiling. Both are true, and neither alone answers
"how tall is this building."

## 1. The thesis

A binding commitment to a minimum number of parking stalls per thousand square feet of
rentable space, combined with a maximum walk from the furthest stall to the entrance,
applied to a parcel of known size, determines the building's height ceiling before any
other design decision is made. The geometry comes first.

Two things make this a real claim rather than a truism.

The first is that the parking commitment is *ours*, not the municipality's. Almost every
town we build in has a parking minimum in its zoning bylaw, and almost every one of them
would let us build less parking than we intend to. Several would actively encourage it.
Our governing agreement binds us to more than the town asks, and it binds us in a way we
cannot relax on a particular site even if the town offers. That inverts the usual
relationship between a developer and a parking requirement, in which the requirement is an
imposition to be negotiated down.

The second is that we let the constraint decide the building rather than the other way
round. The conventional sequence maximises the floor area the zoning permits and then finds
somewhere to put the cars. Ours starts from the cars, and where the arithmetic produces a
fraction of a floor, we drop the fraction. We do not lower the parking to capture the
remainder. Over-building against a fixed constraint produces a larger asset without a larger
return, and a larger asset on the same land is not an improvement — it is the same income
spread over more building to construct, condition, and maintain.

## 2. The problem, in the reader's terms

Anybody who has tried to add a second car to a suburban house knows this arithmetic
already.

The question feels like it is about preference — do we want two cars — and it is not. It is
about the driveway. The lot is a certain width. The driveway is a certain width. Two cars
need somewhere to sit where each can leave without moving the other, and where neither
blocks the footpath. Depending on the lot, the answer is yes, or yes but only in tandem, or
no. No amount of wanting a second car changes it, and the homeowner who buys the car first
and works out the parking afterward ends up with a car on the street and a neighbour's
complaint.

A commercial building is the same problem with the numbers larger and the stakes higher. The
building generates a demand for stalls in proportion to how much space it rents out. The
stalls take land. The land is finite and was expensive. And there is a second constraint the
homeowner does not face: the stall has to be close enough to the door that the person using
it does not resent the walk. A stall four hundred metres away technically exists and
practically does not.

Work forward from the building and you discover the parking problem late, when the design is
committed. Work backward from the parking and the building's size is the answer rather than
the assumption.

## 3. What a parking ratio actually is

The vocabulary here is ordinary in the property industry and opaque outside it, so it is
worth a plain account.

A **parking ratio** is the number of stalls a building provides for each thousand square
feet of space it rents out. It is written as a ratio — four to one means four stalls per
thousand square feet — and it is the standard way both municipalities and tenants express
how much parking a building has. The Institute of Transportation Engineers maintains the
reference work the industry uses to estimate how much parking different building types
actually generate demand for [ite-parking-generation-5].

The denominator matters more than people expect. It is **net leasable area**, not the gross
size of the building. A building's gross area includes the structure, the corridors, the
lobby, the stairs, the mechanical rooms, and the shared washrooms. Net leasable area is the
part a tenant actually rents and pays for. Because the ratio is applied to the smaller
number, how you define net leasable area directly changes how many stalls you owe — and it
is where a developer wanting to build more building will push, since reclassifying a
thousand square feet out of net leasable removes several stalls' worth of land obligation.
Our position is the opposite of that pressure: hold the stall count and argue instead that
shared amenity space — the lobby, the tenant lounge — is not net leasable in the first
place, because a tenant is not renting it.

A **stall** is not an abstraction either. Ours are full-size at nine feet by twenty, with
separate allocation for accessible stalls, electric-vehicle charging, and motorcycles. A
stall also needs a drive aisle to reach it and a footpath to leave it by, so the land a
stall consumes is meaningfully more than the stall itself. This is why parking ratios convert
into acres so quickly, and why the acre is the unit that ends up governing the building.

It is worth noting that the academic literature on parking runs in almost the opposite
direction to our practice. The most influential modern work on the subject argues that
municipal minimum parking requirements are set far too high, are rarely grounded in
evidence, and impose large hidden costs on cities and on the people who do not drive
[shoup-2011-high-cost-of-free-parking] [manville-shoup-2005-parking-people-cities]
[willson-2013-parking-reform-made-easy]. We think that critique is largely correct about
dense, transit-served cities, and we would apply it there. Our sites are the case it does not
reach, for a reason given in section five, and we would rather state the tension plainly than
write as though the literature agreed with us.

## 4. The arithmetic

Four inputs, and then the division.

**The stall obligation.** Our governing agreement binds the office buildings to a minimum of
three stalls per thousand square feet of net leasable area. In practice the design works
toward four, and four is the figure published per building class: four stalls per thousand
for both office classes, with the lighter classes carrying their own lower ratios — three for
the single-storey retail component, which pays more rent for it, and one for light-industrial
space, which generates far less daily visiting.

Both numbers are real and they do different jobs, so it is worth being exact about which is
which. Three is the floor: the binding commitment, the number below which the building may
not go. Four is the target: what the national tenants these buildings are built for actually
require, and what the design is drawn to. **The outer ceiling — the tallest building a given
parcel could support — is the one computed from the binding minimum of three**, because that
is the figure a reader can actually hold a building to. The published prototype heights are
lower than that ceiling, because they are drawn to the four-stall target instead. The last
part of this section explains why that direction is the right way round and not a mistake.

**The walk.** No stall sits more than seventy-five metres from its building entrance. This
is the constraint that surprises people, and it is often the one that actually binds. Parking
extends outward from the building in tiers, and each tier is further away; past a certain
point the land is still there and the useful parking is not. It is also why the buildings are
spread across a site and blended into the parking field rather than clustered together —
clustering puts a larger share of the stalls in the distant tiers, which users notice
immediately.

**The land.** Our development sites run between roughly four and thirteen acres. That is not
a large site by the standards of a downtown block assembly, and it is the actual constraint
rather than a planning nicety.

**The conversion.** From our test sites and our work with parking consultants, the rough
planning guide is that one acre of land supports about one floor of office, and one level of
below-ground parking supports about one more. That is explicitly a rough guide for plot
planning, not a formula, and it is the number a reader should treat with the most caution of
anything in this paper.

Put those together and the building comes out of the division. The acreage available to the
office component — which is not the whole site, since the remainder carries the other
buildings and the landscaping — converts to roughly that many floors of office. Where the
division leaves a fraction, the fraction is dropped and the leftover land is filled with
single-storey retail or light-industrial space rather than with a partial office floor the
parking cannot support. Where more height is wanted, there are only three levers: reduce the
parking ratio, add below-ground parking, or let the walk get longer. Nothing else moves the
answer.

That geometry is what separates our two office building classes, and it is worth being exact
about what does and does not distinguish them, because it is not the stall ratio. A
**Professional Centre** and a **Suburban Office** are both bound to the same governing
minimum described above. What differs is whether any of the required parking is allowed to go
underground. A Professional Centre takes all of its parking on the surface, and working to the
four-stall target with surface parking alone, holding the seventy-five-metre walk, it proves
difficult to get past five floors — which is where that class's ceiling comes from. A Suburban
Office adds up to one and a half levels of below-ground parking within the building's own
footprint. That below-ground allowance buys roughly its own equivalent in floors above ground,
on a somewhat larger parcel, which is how the same ratio supports six to nine floors instead of
three to five. The two classes are, geometrically, one calculation with a single switch thrown:
surface parking only, or surface plus a below-ground allowance.

Nine is not purely a parking calculation for the Suburban Office ceiling: it is also close to
where the structural and building-services consultants place a natural breakpoint, for
elevator provision and emergency travel distances, and the parking geometry independently says
the same class cannot realistically exceed nine or ten floors. The two constraints agree, which
is part of why the ceiling is set there.

One honest gap belongs here rather than being smoothed over. Exactly where a Professional
Centre's range ends and a Suburban Office's begins — whether the practical cutoff sits at four
floors or five — is not a fixed rule we can point to. It is a live design question, still being
worked out against the parking geometry above and against the building codes of each
jurisdiction we build in, and we would rather say so than publish a boundary that reads more
settled than it is.

The governing agreement's own text contemplates the taller building going to fifteen floors.
The geometry says that is not achievable under the same ratio and walk limit. We treat the
fifteen as a figure the geometry has overtaken, and the working ceiling is nine.

Now the consequence of the two ratios, stated plainly because it cuts against the direction a
reader might expect. Designing to four stalls per thousand rather than three means providing
about a third more parking for the same rentable area — which means the same parcel supports
*fewer* floors, not more. The published ceilings of five and nine floors are therefore where
the four-stall working target lands, and they sit below the ceiling the three-stall binding
minimum would permit on the same land. That is arithmetic on the two ratios rather than a
measurement, and it is worth understanding correctly: the higher target is a self-imposed
restriction on how much building we get from a site, not a licence for more of it.

## 5. Why the ratio is set above what towns require

A developer providing more parking than the municipality asks for is unusual enough to need
an explanation, and ours is specific rather than philosophical.

The first half is about the markets. In the regional markets we build in, the private
automobile is how nearly everyone arrives — tenants, their staff, and their customers. There
is no rapid transit, and our site-selection criteria mean there is not going to be: these are
towns with a large-format retail node and no rail. This is our own observation of how these
places actually work, not a claim inherited from how the markets are defined. We would
genuinely support lower parking ratios where rapid transit exists, and we would build to them
there. We do not have that case.

The second half is about what happens when a developer gets this wrong, which we have
watched. A development is built with relaxed parking — often with the town's active
agreement, since the town would prefer landscaping to asphalt and so would we. The
pre-leasing goes well; strong tenants, including national ones, sign. Then, around the fifth
year, they do not renew. The reason given is not rent. It is that their people and their
customers cannot park, or do not feel safe walking from where they parked, or cannot
circulate through an undersized lot at the times of day when it matters. The development loses
the prestige that its anchor tenants gave it, word of mouth in a small community does the
rest, and the tenants who remain are in a position to demand a lower rent at renewal. The
parking saving was realised at construction and paid for over the following decade.

That is why the ratio is set by what the national tenants say they require for their own
businesses to work, rather than by the minimum a bylaw will accept. And it is why the
commitment sits in the governing agreement rather than in a design guideline — so that it
cannot be relaxed site by site under commercial pressure, including by us.

One thing this section is not arguing. Providing generous parking is not itself a virtue, and
a correctly sized parking field laid out as bare asphalt is a worse outcome than a smaller
one done well. The stall count travels with a design requirement: oversized drive lanes and
footpaths through the parking field, and planting substantial enough to shade both people and
cars. The count and the landscaping are one standard, not two line items, and there is a
long-term ambition attached to it — if rapid transit ever arrives in one of these markets, a
meaningful share of the stalls becomes reclaimable for landscaping and rooftop solar rather
than remaining asphalt forever.

There is a further consequence that belongs to the same geometry. A building sized backward
from its parking, with the fractional floor dropped rather than captured, is a smaller
building than the zoning would allow. Less building for the same tenants means less to
construct, less to heat and cool, and less to maintain. We treat that as the same discipline
as the geometry of sustainability applied elsewhere in these buildings, rather than as a
separate environmental initiative: the constraint that decides the height also decides how
much building there is.

## What this changes for the reader

The change is that a building's plausible height becomes something a reader can check for
themselves, on any commercial property, with public information.

The check is: find the site's acreage, find the building's net leasable area, count the
stalls, and divide. Does the building provide at least three stalls per thousand square feet
of net leasable area — the binding floor? How far is the furthest stall from the door? And
does the number of floors look consistent with the acreage, at roughly a floor per acre plus
whatever is below ground? A building that fails those tests has either less parking than its
tenants will want, or a longer walk than they will tolerate, or has resolved the tension some
other way that is worth asking about.

That check works on buildings that have nothing to do with us, which is most of its value. It
is also the reason this paper runs its arithmetic off the binding three rather than the
working four: a reader applying our own advice to one of our own buildings should be checking
against the number we are actually committed to, not the number we aim at.

The trade-offs are real. Parking at these ratios is a deliberate over-provision by the
standards of modern planning practice, and the academic literature cited earlier would call
it a cost imposed on the site and the town. It consumes land that could be building or
landscape. It caps the buildings at heights that a taller-is-better view would call
conservative. And it rests on a forecast — that the private automobile remains how people
reach these places for the length of a building's life — which is a forecast and not a fact.
If that forecast is wrong, we will have built parking fields that no longer earn their land,
which is why the reclamation ambition above exists at all.

## An open invitation

The question underneath this paper is not really a question about buildings.

To parking-policy and transportation researchers: the honest open question is whether a
fixed, high ratio remains the right target across the fifty-year life of a building, when
parking demand itself is moving. Remote and hybrid working patterns have changed weekday peak
occupancy in office buildings in ways that were not modelled beforehand. Vehicle automation,
if it arrives at
scale, changes the relationship between a trip and a stored car more fundamentally than any
policy could. We have designed for the demand we observe now, with an intention to reclaim
stalls later, and we do not know whether that is the right hedge or an expensive one. We would
rather have that argued with us than assume it.

To the reform literature specifically: we have cited work that argues our practice is wrong,
because we think it is right about most places and we are not sure where the boundary of the
exception is. The most useful contribution anyone could make is a characterisation of *which*
markets genuinely require high ratios and which merely have them out of habit — a test, not a
verdict. We claim our markets are on the exception side. We would like that claim tested.

To municipal planners in the towns we intend to build in: we are in the unusual position of
building more parking than you are asking for, which may cut against your own objectives for
your town centre. We would rather have that conversation early than be surprised by it at
permit stage, and we would rather contribute to a transit case in these markets than simply
outlast the lack of one.

And to anyone who has operated a development where parking was relaxed and tenants later
left: the non-renewal pattern described in section five is an observation from our own
experience, and it is doing a great deal of work in this argument. If the pattern is more
complicated than we describe — if there are cases where relaxed parking worked in a
comparable market — that is the finding most likely to change what we build.

To structural engineers and building-code specialists across the jurisdictions we build in:
the honest gap named in section four — exactly where a Professional Centre's range gives way
to a Suburban Office's — is unresolved on our side too. We would welcome help settling it
against real local code, rather than treating the four-or-five-floor question as a detail to
be fixed later.

## Conclusion

Parking geometry sets a building's height ceiling. Three stalls per thousand square feet of
net leasable area is the binding floor; four is the target the buildings are actually drawn
to, because it is what the tenants they are built for require; no stall sits further than
seventy-five metres from the door; and one acre of land supports about one floor of office,
with a level of below-ground parking buying about one more. Divide, drop the fraction, and the
building's height is the answer rather than the ambition. That is the whole of this paper's
claim, and its narrowness is deliberate: it says nothing about how a building is financed,
which is a separate question with its own answer. Geometry decides how tall the building can
be. Something else decides whether it gets built well.

## Data and reproducibility

Three kinds of figure appear in this paper and they carry different weight.

The stall obligations, the seventy-five-metre walk limit, and the stall dimensions are terms
of a governing agreement and of the prototype specification drawn against it. They are
commitments, not measurements, and a reader relying on them should read the agreement rather
than this summary. The two office parking figures are worth restating here because the
difference matters and is easy to lose: three stalls per thousand square feet of net leasable
area is the binding minimum the agreement imposes, and four is the ratio the buildings are
designed to and published at. The arithmetic in this paper runs off the binding three, and
says so where it does, because a reader checking a building should be checking against the
commitment rather than the ambition.

The conversion between land and floors — one acre to about one floor of office, one
below-ground level to about one more — comes from our own test-site plot planning with parking
consultants. It is described in our own material as a very rough guide and should be read that
way. It is the least precise figure in this paper and the one most likely to move as real
sites are planned.

The published prototype ranges — three to five floors for the shorter office building, six to
nine for the taller — are design conclusions reached with structural and building-services
consultants, not observations of built buildings. No Woodfine Building exists, so there is no
measured stall count, walk distance, floor count, or occupancy anywhere behind this paper. The
non-renewal pattern at under-parked developments described in section five is our own
observation from prior work in these markets rather than a study, and we have not attempted to
quantify it.

The academic literature cited runs largely against our practice, which is why we have cited
it. Those works are published and available through their publishers, and a reader who wants
to weigh our position against the mainstream view should read them rather than our
characterisation of them. No independent party has reviewed the geometry described here,
verified the conversion guide, or confirmed the claims made for it.
