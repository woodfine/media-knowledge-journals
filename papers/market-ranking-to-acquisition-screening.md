## 1. The question

An institutional site-selection committee evaluating commercial real estate across a large
geography faces a scale problem before it faces an underwriting problem: there are far more
candidate markets than any team can manually survey in useful depth. A geographic
classification methodology that identifies which locations have the retail composition and
geographic isolation of a genuine regional commercial center can solve the scale problem —
narrowing thousands of candidates down to a manageable, defensible short list. It cannot, by
itself, solve the underwriting problem, because market structure and asset-level economics
are two different questions answered by two different kinds of analysis.

The risk this paper addresses is not that the classification methodology is wrong — it is
that a composite score, presented without the right framing, invites exactly the wrong
reading. A number that ranks markets looks, to an unfamiliar reader, like a ranking of
investment merit. This paper asks: how should a market-structure classification be presented
and used so that it does the real, valuable work of narrowing a universe of candidates,
without inviting the reader to mistake a structural classification for a return forecast?

## 2. What we found

**A market-structure classification built purely from retail composition and geographic
isolation — with no population, income, or financial figure in the qualification test at
all — produces a real, defensible short list from an otherwise unmanageable universe of
candidates.** The classification asks a narrow, observable question at each candidate
location: does this market have the retail-anchor composition (specific store formats
present together) and the geographic isolation from other qualifying markets that
characterizes a genuine regional commercial center, as distinct from a suburb whose catchment
happens to overlap a larger nearby city. This is a structural, retail-composition question,
not an economic one — and answering it at scale, across a large geography, is exactly the
kind of task a consistent, mechanically-applied rule does better than a manual survey.

**The classification uses three distinct qualification tiers, and a market either meets one
of them or it is not classified at all — there is no partial credit and no override.** A
market qualifies at the strongest tier when it combines a full grocery/general-merchandise
anchor with at least two of several destination-format categories (hardware, price-club,
lifestyle, electronics, sport) across its constituent retail clusters. A market qualifies at
a second tier when it has the grocery and hardware anchors present with real multi-site depth
but lacks the broader destination-format mix. A third tier applies the same test to markets
that are otherwise isolated from any other qualifying market beyond the standard geographic
band. A market meeting none of these three gates is simply absent from the classification —
never force-included and never scored by exception.

**Selecting the highest-scoring markets within each continent, with no per-country quota,
produces materially different real counts by region — and the difference is a disclosed fact
about where retail infrastructure of this kind actually exists, not a flaw in the
methodology.** Applying the classification across North America currently identifies more
than eleven hundred qualifying markets; applying it across Europe currently identifies
roughly six hundred fifty. A published, curated list draws the highest-scoring several hundred
from each continent's own qualifying pool independently — the two continents are never
combined into one ranking, and a market's score is never compared across continents. Where a
continent's full qualifying pool is smaller than the list's target count, every qualifying
market is published; nothing is padded in to reach a round number, and nothing that failed
the qualification gate is added to fill the gap.

**Population and consumer-spending figures are deliberately excluded from the scoring and
selection logic entirely, for a specific, tested reason: population is negatively correlated
with the composition-and-isolation score in every geography tested.** Selecting on
population, rather than on retail composition and isolation, would systematically favor dense
metro-fringe suburbs whose catchment simply overlaps a major city's own population, rather
than genuinely distinct regional commercial centers — the opposite of what this
classification is built to identify. Population and spending figures remain available as
separate, purely descriptive context for a market that has already qualified; they play no
role in whether a market qualifies or how qualifying markets are ordered.

## 3. How the classification actually works

The methodology evaluates each candidate market's constituent retail clusters against the
three-tier composition gate described in §2, using only observable, verifiable facts about
which anchor store formats are present and how those formats are geographically distributed
— never a survey, a forecast, or an economic projection. Markets that qualify are then ordered
within their continent by a composite score reflecting composition strength and geographic
isolation, with no per-country budget of any kind; a small per-country floor exists solely to
ensure no country with genuinely qualifying markets is entirely excluded from the published
list by chance, and does not otherwise affect ordering.

For an acquisition-screening application specifically, we recommend the classification be
used exactly as a coarse filter, in three concrete steps that keep the classification's real
value while avoiding its real limit. First, use the classification to reduce an intractably
large universe of candidate geographies to the several hundred markets per continent that
meet a genuine regional-commercial-center composition test — a real, defensible narrowing
step that would otherwise require an infeasible amount of manual survey work. Second, treat
every market that clears this filter as equally eligible for a closer look, not as pre-ranked
by investment merit — the classification's own ordering reflects composition and isolation
strength, not asset economics, and should not be read as an implicit priority order for site
visits or underwriting effort. Third, apply ordinary real-estate underwriting — rent levels,
comparable transactions, construction cost, entitlement risk, a specific return analysis — only
after a market clears the structural filter, and treat that underwriting, not the
classification score, as the actual basis for any acquisition decision.

## 4. What it changes

For an acquisition-screening committee, the practical change is being explicit, in writing,
about which step in the process the classification actually performs. A committee that treats
a high composite score as itself an investment signal has skipped the underwriting step
entirely and substituted a structural classification for it — a specific, nameable reasoning
error this paper exists to help a committee avoid, not a hypothetical risk. Naming the
distinction plainly — market structure, not asset economics — lets a committee use the
classification for exactly the scale problem it is built to solve, while keeping the actual
investment decision where it belongs: in ordinary underwriting, applied after the
classification has already done its narrowing work.

## 5. Where this could be wrong

**The classification measures retail composition and geographic isolation only — it makes no
claim, implicit or explicit, about rent levels, capitalization rates, construction cost, or
any specific return.** A market that scores highly on this classification can still be a poor
acquisition at the wrong price, in the wrong entitlement environment, or against the wrong
underwriting assumptions; nothing in this framework substitutes for that analysis.

**The classification's underlying data has real, disclosed gaps in some geographies.**
Catchment population and consumer-spending context figures — which play no role in
qualification or ordering, but are useful descriptive context once a market has qualified —
are currently unavailable for several countries' markets due to real gaps in the underlying
census and spending data sources, not an error in how the classification itself is computed.

**A classification built on current retail composition describes the present, not the
future.** A market's qualification status reflects the retail anchors present in the data at
the time of the most recent refresh; it says nothing about how a market's composition may
change, and carries no forecast of future retail entry or exit.

## 6. Conclusion

A market-structure classification built purely from retail composition and geographic
isolation solves a real scale problem for institutional site selection — narrowing an
otherwise unmanageable universe of candidate markets to a defensible, several-hundred-market
short list per continent, using a consistent, mechanically-applied rule rather than a manual
survey. What it cannot do, and what this paper is explicit it does not claim to do, is stand
in for asset-level underwriting. The correct use of the classification is as the first,
narrowing step in a screening process — clearing the structural filter earns a market a
closer look, never a purchase recommendation, and every acquisition decision still rests on
ordinary underwriting applied after that filter, not on the classification score itself.

## 7. Claims and what would count against them

**Screening-value claim.** The three-tier composition-and-isolation classification narrows
a large universe of candidate markets to a defensible short list that a manual survey could
not produce at comparable scale and consistency.

**Non-valuation claim.** The classification's composite score carries no information about
asset-level economics — rent, capitalization rate, construction cost, or return — and should
never be read as ranking investment merit.

| Test | What it checks | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Qualification-gate consistency | Whether the same three-tier composition test is applied identically across every candidate market, with no manual override | Verified in the current implementation: no exceptions or manual inclusions exist |
| Population-independence check | Whether population or consumer-spend figures influence qualification or ordering | Verified: neither field participates in scoring or selection in the current implementation |
| Score-vs-return correlation | Whether the composite score correlates with any real, measured asset-level return | Not tested — this paper makes no such claim and the underlying methodology was not designed to measure it |

The screening-value claim is falsified if the classification is shown to systematically
admit markets lacking genuine regional-commercial-center composition, or to systematically
exclude markets that plainly have it. The non-valuation claim is falsified if the composite
score is shown to correlate with real asset-level returns in a way that would justify using
it as an investment ranking rather than a screening filter — a test this paper does not
attempt and explicitly flags as unperformed.

### Appendix A — Current published counts (representative, subject to refresh)

North America: approximately 1,100 qualifying markets identified; several hundred highest-
scoring markets published per the current selection cutoff. Europe: approximately 650
qualifying markets identified; because the qualifying pool is smaller than the selection
target, every qualifying market is published. The two continents are scored and selected
entirely independently.
