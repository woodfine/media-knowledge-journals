# One Rulebook, Four Statutes

*Universal Governing Bylaws across four national vehicles — and the places where the approximation fractures*

> Nothing in this paper constitutes an offer to sell, or a solicitation of an offer to buy,
> any interest in a Woodfine direct-hold solution; any offering is made only by the
> applicable Private Placement Memorandum. Statements marked "planned," "intended,"
> "targeted," "may," or "expected" are forward-looking and subject to change. Full
> disclosures appear at the end of this paper.

Working Paper WCP-016 · v0.1.0 · CC BY 4.0

Our thesis is that the right way to run the same investment in four countries is to stop trying
to run the same *vehicle* in four countries. Each of the four has its own long-established legal
form for holding income property on behalf of investors, each of those forms is familiar to the
local regulator, the local tax authority, local counsel and local investors, and each already
works. What we lay on top of them, identically in each country, is the rulebook: one set of
governing provisions — the **Universal Governing Bylaws** — written into whatever instrument
that country's form uses, whether that is a partnership agreement, corporate bylaws or a trust
deed.

The result is intended to be approximately the same investor protections in Canada, the United
States, Spain and Mexico, without anybody having invented a new kind of legal entity. The word
doing the real work in that sentence is *approximately*, and this paper exists to say precisely
where the approximation holds and where it breaks. The places where it breaks have a name in
this company's own vocabulary — the **Jurisdictional Fracture** — and they are not a caveat
appended at the end. They are the content. Section 4 names each one specifically, because a
reader comparing the same investment offered through two different national vehicles is entitled
to know exactly which rights are identical and which are not.

One honest framing note before the argument. Of the four vehicles, one exists: the Canadian
limited partnership. The United States, Spain and Mexico vehicles are planned and not yet
formed, which means their governing instruments have not been written. So the uniformity claimed
here is at this stage a design intention verified against three countries' statutes, not a
comparison of four real documents that anybody can read side by side. That is a meaningful
limitation on the claim and it recurs throughout.

## 1. The thesis

The same core protections sit on top of four different national legal forms, each of which keeps
its own country's familiar shape. What makes that a claim rather than a platitude is that the
protections are specific, they are written into the governing instrument rather than described in
marketing material, and several of them are placed beyond the reach of a later amendment.

Our position is that this is strictly better than the alternative it replaces. The alternative is
to design one bespoke, jurisdiction-spanning structure and then persuade four countries to
accommodate it. That approach produces an entity nobody locally recognises: a tax authority that
has to decide what it is, a regulator with no precedent to apply to it, local counsel who cannot
advise on it without qualification, and an investor who cannot compare it to anything they have
seen before. Every one of those is a cost, and none of them buys the investor anything.

The reason the rulebook can travel while the entity cannot is that the two do different work.
What a country's statute fixes is the *form* — who holds title, who governs, what the instrument
is called, whether it must be listed, how the tax flows. What a contract can fix is the
*conduct* — what the money may be invested in, what may be charged, what must be distributed,
who has to agree before the rules change. The second list is where almost all of an investor's
real protection lives, and it is portable. The first list is not portable, and pretending
otherwise is the mistake this design is built to avoid.

## 2. The problem, in the reader's terms

Some vocabulary first, because none of it is obvious and all of it is load-bearing.

A **governing instrument** is the contract that sets out how a pooled investment actually
operates: what it may buy, who runs it, what they may be paid, what has to be distributed, and
how any of that can be changed. Its name changes by country and by form — a partnership
agreement, a company's bylaws, a trust deed — and the name matters less than the fact that it is
the document a court would read if there were ever a dispute. It is the rulebook, and an
investor's real protections live in it rather than in a brochure.

A **flow-through vehicle** is one that is not taxed on its own income; the income passes through
to the investors, who are taxed once, on their own account. Without it, income earned by a
property is taxed at the entity and taxed again when it reaches the investor — the same money
taxed twice. Every serious country has invented at least one such form for real estate, and the
forms have different names and different conditions attached: a limited partnership in Canada
and the United States, a SOCIMI in Spain, a FIBRA in Mexico.

A **general partner** is the entity that manages a limited partnership and bears its
liabilities; the limited partners put up capital, take the income, and are barred from
participating in management, which is the price of their limited liability. An **Administrator**
is the rough equivalent role under Spanish and Mexican forms, and "rough" is doing some work in
that sentence, as Section 4 explains.

Now the shape of the problem. A company operating in several countries does not invent a single
global corporate entity and ask each country to recognise it. It incorporates a local subsidiary
in each country, in that country's own standard form, and then imposes its own group policies on
all of them — the same code of conduct, the same approval limits, the same reporting. The local
form is whatever the local statute provides. The policy is the company's own, and it is the same
everywhere. That is exactly the division of labour here, applied to an investment vehicle rather
than an operating subsidiary.

**A count that is easy to confuse, so it is worth separating now.** This paper is about *four*
national investment vehicles: Canada, the United States, Spain, Mexico. A reader who works
through this company's other material will also meet a different number — *nine* planned
development jurisdictions, which adds Poland, the United Kingdom, Italy, the Nordics and a
grouping described as New Europe to the four above. Those nine are where buildings are planned
to be developed. The four are where investment vehicles are planned to be constituted. They are
not the same list, they are not the same kind of thing, and neither number is a revision of the
other.

## 3. What the Universal Governing Bylaws actually contain

A framework of protective clauses is a phrase that means nothing until the clauses are named. The
following are the provisions of the established Canada vehicle's own governing agreement, and
they are the model intended to be reproduced in the other three instruments as each vehicle is
formed. Where a provision is specific to Canada today, that is said.

**Capital may only go into a defined category of investment.** The agreement does not authorise
the vehicle to buy property generally. It confines capital to a **Qualified Investment**: a
defined category tied to one or more contiguous parcels supporting a professional centre in a
regional market, adjacent to major national retailers. An investor is therefore not subscribing
to a manager's general judgement about real estate; they are subscribing to a narrow mandate that
the manager cannot unilaterally widen.

**Interest cannot be capitalised.** Capitalising interest means adding the interest owed during
construction to the loan's principal instead of paying it as it accrues. It is legal, common,
and it has one consistent effect: debt grows while the cost stays out of current income. The
agreement prohibits it, so interest appears as a cost in the period it is incurred.

**No fee may be calculated by reference to net asset value.** The agreement prohibits the
partnership from paying any person any fee, reward or inducement calculated by reference to the
net asset value of its assets, or to a change in that value. It also restricts who may charge the
partnership at all: apart from the retained advisor's fees, no subsidiary or affiliate of the
promoter is entitled to receive fees from the partnership. Another of our papers is entirely
about why the base a fee is calculated on matters more than the rate; this is the clause that
puts it beyond the reach of a later fee redesign. It is, today, a term of the Canadian
agreement, and the other three instruments are not yet written.

**Changing the rules needs a supermajority, and some rules cannot be changed at all.** An
amendment requires a **special resolution** — seventy-five per cent of votes cast at a properly
constituted meeting, or the written consent of holders of seventy-five per cent of all
outstanding units. That threshold sits well above a simple majority, so an amendment requires
broad consensus rather than a plurality of whoever turns up. More importantly, a defined list of
changes sits outside what any vote can reach: no amendment may reduce any limited partner's
interest, change a partner's voting rights or liability, change how income, loss and tax
attributes are allocated between the partners, deny or reduce a tax deduction a partner would
otherwise have, or reduce or eliminate the partnership's obligation to issue the escrowed
promoter equity. Amending the amendment clause itself requires unanimity. A protection that a
supermajority can delete is worth less than one it cannot, and the distinction between the two
lists is the most underrated provision in this rulebook.

**Existing equity holders get first refusal on the vehicle's own debt.** When the vehicle issues
its own mortgage debentures to fund the next construction phase, existing equity investors have a
right of first refusal — the chance to buy into that debt before it is offered outside. The same
investor can hold both halves of the same buildings' capital structure if they want to.

**Income has to be distributed, not retained at discretion.** Where a distribution is declared,
at least ninety per cent of the fiscal year's distributable income is paid to limited partners
within ninety days of the year end. Until cumulative distributions equal the whole of what
limited partners paid for their units, the remaining tenth is retained for partnership purposes;
after that, it is applied to redeeming debentures until they are fully redeemed. There is no
preferred class, no hurdle to clear before ordinary units participate, and no accumulated
preferred entitlement — all units of the same asset receive the same amount each.

**New borrowing is gated on income already earned.** Further debentures may not be issued if
doing so would take the vehicle's interest coverage below 1.20 times, measured across the vehicle
as a whole, absent a special resolution. The financing sequence this belongs to is the subject of
another of our papers.

**Developer profit sits behind investor capital.** The promoter's equity is issued as
compensation rather than subscribed for at the price limited partners paid, and it is held in
escrow. Voting rights are retained throughout, but the units do not become freely disposable
until limited partners have recovered one hundred per cent of their contributed capital, or
another defined liquidity event occurs.

**Governance is majority-independent by contract.** In Canada, the general partner is bound by a
unanimous shareholder agreement requiring a majority of its board to be arm's-length, independent
directors. The planned Mexican Administrator carries the same intention, with a majority of its
own board intended to be independent directors dealing at arm's length with the group, the
promoter and the advisor.

One plain statement to close this section rather than to qualify it. Every provision above is a
term of one agreement governing one vehicle. The uniformity across four is a design intention.
When the other three instruments are written, a reader will be able to check it, and until then
the honest description is that three of four are planned.

## 4. Where the Jurisdictional Fracture actually shows up

This is the part of the paper that is worth a reader's time, because it is where the uniform
story stops being uniform. Each fracture below is specific and checkable against the relevant
country's own statute, and none of them is a drafting problem that better lawyers would solve.
They are places where a national statute simply requires something different.

**The vocabulary itself does not travel.** "Public but not listed" is a real, ordinary status in
Canada and the United States: a vehicle can be a reporting issuer, with all the filing
obligations that carries, without any of its units trading on an exchange. That combination is
what both of those vehicles are designed around. Spanish market terminology draws the line
differently. *Cotizada* — listed — is the legal definition of being a public company, and there
is no separate category for a public-but-unlisted issuer at all. So the single phrase that
describes the whole design in two of the four countries describes nothing in the third. This is
the fracture the name was coined for, and it is a fracture in language that turns out to be a
fracture in law.

**Spain's tax status is conditional on listing, and there is no unlisted path to it.** A SOCIMI's
exemption from Spanish corporate income tax on qualifying rental income and gains depends on
meeting asset, income and distribution tests *and* on maintaining a listing on a regulated
market. The planned answer is a technical listing — admission sufficient to satisfy the legal
requirement, without pursuing the trading volume of a fully liquid public market — with
settlement through Iberclear and continuous disclosure filed with Spain's securities regulator
[cnmv]. It is a real and reasonable answer. It is not the same position as the other three
vehicles occupy, and a Spanish holder's units would be admitted to an exchange and subject to
electronic matching in a way a Canadian holder's units are not. Anyone who prefers the unlisted
position cannot have it in Spain at any price.

**Mexico requires a bank to hold the title, and the group cannot hold that role.** A FIBRA must
have a licensed Mexican banking institution as its *fiduciario* — its trustee — and that bank
holds legal title. This is statutory, not a structuring choice. The planned design puts a
Technical Committee in place to instruct the bank on development, leasing and fee decisions, with
the bank mandated to follow those instructions rather than exercise its own discretion over the
real-estate business. That is a sensible arrangement and it is *not* the same governance shape as
a general partner that both governs and is bound to investors by a partnership agreement. An
investor comparing the two is comparing a vehicle whose governing entity is a group subsidiary
with a vehicle in which a third-party bank holds legal title and takes instructions. Both are
defensible. They are not the same thing, and the difference is not one the Bylaws can erase.

**The transfer mechanism is not the same mechanism.** In Canada and the planned United States
vehicle, the governing entity records a transfer once the transferee completes the required
transfer documentation — a ministerial step, not an approval, which is the subject of another of
our papers and is not restated here. In Spain and Mexico, eligibility is enforced directly at
the depositary or register, with no governing-entity step at all. The *outcome* is similar — a
holder does not need discretionary permission to sell — and the *machinery* is different, which
matters the moment something goes wrong, because a holder's counterparty in a dispute would be a
different institution in each case.

**The kind of price a holder can see is different in each country.** This is the fracture most
likely to surprise someone comparing two of these vehicles. A Canadian holder has no published
quote at all: units are not listed, they transfer over the counter at a bilaterally agreed
price, and the reference points are appraisals rather than trades. A Spanish holder, on a
technical listing, has an exchange price, produced by electronic matching on a market
deliberately not pursued for liquidity — which may be a thin price rather than an informative
one. A Mexican holder of a Private FIBRA's certificates sees something different again: public
market data for such a vehicle shows volume as zero and price as not applicable, because there is
no listed quote to report, and in its place the certificates carry a **Reference Value** anchored
to the trust's net asset value, published daily to the central depository by a certified pricing
vendor on the basis of periodic independent property appraisals. Three holders of what is
presented as the same investment therefore read three different kinds of number when they ask
what their holding is worth: no number, a thin market number, and a daily appraisal-derived
number. None of them is wrong. They are not comparable, and a reader should not treat them as
comparable.

There is a related point that deserves care rather than a slogan. The Canadian agreement
prohibits any fee calculated by reference to net asset value, while the Mexican design has a
net-asset-value figure published daily by statutory design. Those are compatible — one governs
what may be *charged*, the other governs how a certificate is *priced* — and we are not going to
pretend they conflict. But a reader who has absorbed the argument against net-asset-value
dependence should know that in one of the four jurisdictions a net-asset-value figure sits at the
centre of the instrument's own pricing machinery, and should ask what the equivalent fee
prohibition looks like in the Mexican trust deed when it is written. We cannot answer that today,
because it is not written.

**Local statute binds the business, not only the paperwork.** Mexico's fiscal transparency under
Article 188 of its income tax law carries conditions on what the trust actually does: a property
the trust develops is intended to be held for lease for at least four years after completion
rather than sold, and at least seventy per cent of trust assets are intended to be invested in
real estate destined for lease. Spain's SOCIMI exemption similarly depends on asset, income and
distribution tests as well as the listing. These are not documentary formalities. They constrain
disposal decisions and portfolio composition, and they are not identical across the four, which
means the same investment discipline is bounded differently in each country by something the
Bylaws did not choose.

**And the instrument is a different kind of thing in each place.** In Canada and the United
States, a holder owns units of a limited partnership. In Spain, shares of a listed company. In
Mexico, trust participation certificates — a beneficial interest in a trust whose legal title to
the property sits with a bank, through subsidiaries that each hold one site. Those are different
legal characters with different remedies, different insolvency treatment and different tax
analysis for the holder, not four labels for one thing. The economic and governance design is
intended to be functionally equivalent. The legal character is not equivalent and cannot be made
so.

One fracture we cannot yet report on properly, and would rather name than skip: the comparative
minimum distribution floors. The at-least-ninety-per-cent rule described in Section 3 is the
Canadian agreement's. Spain's and Mexico's flow-through regimes each impose their own statutory
distribution requirements, and their interaction with the Bylaws' own floor is exactly the kind
of place a fracture would appear — a statutory floor above the contractual one would override it,
one below it would leave the contractual one binding. Only Canada's figure is published today.
Anyone comparing two of these vehicles on distribution policy should ask for both numbers rather
than assume the Canadian one travels.

## What this changes for the reader

The first change is a specific list of questions to ask about any investment offered through more
than one national vehicle. Is the instrument the same kind of legal thing in each country — a
share, a partnership unit, a trust certificate? Who holds legal title to the property, and is
that a party the sponsor controls? Is the vehicle required to be listed anywhere, and if so why?
What price can I actually see, and what produces it? And which of the protections I am being
shown are in the local instrument rather than in a group-level description of the programme?
Those five answers will usually reveal more about a cross-border structure than its summary table
does.

The second is a caution against the summary table itself, including ours. A comparison chart
showing the same tick in four columns is answering the question "is the intention the same." It
is not answering "is my legal position the same," and for this structure the honest answer to
the second question is: approximately, with the named exceptions above. A reader who takes the
list of fractures out of this paper and asks about each one by name is doing exactly what we
would want a reader to do.

The third is a straightforward practical point about where to look. Each vehicle's continuous
disclosure is filed with its own country's regulator on its own system — Canada's on SEDAR+
[sedar-plus], the United States' on EDGAR, Spain's with the CNMV [cnmv], Mexico's with the CNBV
[cnbv]. There is no single consolidated place where all four records sit, and there is not going
to be one, because each regulator maintains its own. An investor holding two of these vehicles
would be reading two filing records in two languages under two disclosure regimes.

The trade-offs belong together. Using four familiar local forms means accepting four different
legal characters for the holder's instrument, which is a real cost that a bespoke single vehicle
would have avoided — at the price of being unrecognisable everywhere. The Bylaws can constrain
conduct but cannot override statute, so the fractures above are permanent features rather than
work in progress. Three of the four vehicles do not exist, so three-quarters of the uniformity
claim is currently an intention. And the vehicle that does exist is subject to an active
cease-trade order issued by the British Columbia Securities Commission, arising from delayed
continuous-disclosure filings rather than any finding of fraud or misconduct, and must bring its
filings current and obtain the Commission's revocation before any future offering of its units
can close — which is a reminder that a well-drafted rulebook and a well-kept filing record are
separate accomplishments.

## An open invitation

The questions this paper leaves open are for comparative lawyers rather than for developers.

To comparative securities-law researchers: the interesting question is whether a fifth
jurisdiction could be added while preserving this degree of near-uniformity, or whether four is
close to the practical limit of the approach. Our own suspicion is that the binding constraint is
not the number of countries but the diversity of their flow-through forms: the four in place
already span a partnership, a listed company and a trust, which is most of the available design
space, and a fifth might add nothing new or might break something we have not anticipated.
Nobody inside a company is well placed to judge that about their own structure.

To researchers on the relationship between contract and statute in investor protection: the
design assumption here is that the protections worth having are mostly contractual and therefore
portable, while the things statute fixes are mostly form. We think that is right and we know it
is not obviously right. There is a plausible opposing view — that statutory protections are the
ones that actually get enforced, because a regulator enforces them without an investor having to
sue, and that a contractual protection is only as good as a minority holder's willingness to
litigate. If that view is correct, a structure that maximises contractual protection and accepts
statutory variation has optimised the less useful half. That is a serious objection and we would
rather see it argued than ignore it.

To Mexican and Spanish counsel specifically: the two fractures we are least confident about are
the ones we can least check from here. In Mexico, what the practical limits are on a Technical
Committee's ability to instruct a bank fiduciary when the two disagree, and what a certificate
holder's remedy actually is in that situation. In Spain, whether a technical listing pursued to
satisfy a statutory requirement carries obligations or exposures — market-abuse, disclosure,
price-formation — that a lightly traded admission does not obviously avoid. Both are questions
where being told we are wrong now is worth a great deal more than discovering it later.

To anyone who has drafted an equivalent multi-jurisdiction rulebook: the provision we are most
attached to is the list of matters that no supermajority vote can amend, because a protection a
vote can delete is a weaker protection than it appears. If there is a jurisdiction where such a
list is unenforceable, or a mechanism that achieves the same result more robustly, that is the
most useful thing anyone could tell us.

## Conclusion

One rulebook, applied four ways, fracturing in named places — that is the honest description of
this structure, and the fracture list is the part a reader can use. The portable half is real:
capital confined to a defined mandate, no capitalised interest, no fee calculated on net asset
value, a supermajority to change the rules with a defined set of changes no vote can reach, a
right of first refusal on the vehicle's own debt, a distribution floor, and developer profit held
behind investor capital. The unportable half is equally real: Spain must be listed and has no
category for anything else, Mexico's title must sit with a bank and its property must be held for
lease for years, the transfer machinery differs, the instrument is a different legal creature in
each country, and the kind of price a holder can see differs in all three ways. One vehicle of
the four exists. The other three are planned, their instruments are unwritten, and the uniformity
is therefore an intention rather than something a reader can yet verify. Naming the fractures is
the part of the claim we can stand behind today.
