## 1. The question

A commercial land buyer scouting the growing edge of a metropolitan area regularly encounters
a specific, recognizable retail pattern: a hardware store, an auto-service outlet, and general
trades retail already operating, but no full-format grocery store anywhere nearby. The
intuitive reading of this pattern treats the missing grocery anchor as a negative signal — a
market that retail investment has evaluated and passed over, presumably because the location
lacks something a grocery operator's own site-selection process requires.

This paper argues that intuitive reading is frequently wrong, and wrong in a specific,
identifiable way: it treats the *order* in which retail formats enter a growing market as if
it were random, when it is not. Different retail formats have different minimum viable
rooftop counts — the number of nearby households needed to support that format's business
model — and those thresholds are reached at different points as a fringe area's residential
population grows. A pattern of early-threshold formats without a late-threshold one may
simply describe a market caught mid-sequence, not a market the sequence has skipped. The
question this paper asks: when should a land buyer read a missing grocery anchor as a
sequencing fact rather than a verdict on the location — and what would actually distinguish
the two cases at a real site?

## 2. What we found

**Retail-format entry into a growing market follows a well-documented rooftop-threshold
logic, independent of any single company's specific site-selection algorithm.** A retail
format's minimum viable market size is driven by the population density its particular
business model requires to sustain the sales volume its cost structure needs — a smaller-
format, narrower-assortment store can operate profitably at a lower rooftop count than a
full-format supermarket carrying a much broader inventory and a correspondingly higher fixed
cost base. This is general retail-location economics, observed across many markets and many
retail categories, not a proprietary finding specific to any one company's expansion
criteria.

**Auto-service, hardware, and general trades retail formats typically have lower rooftop
thresholds than full-format grocery anchors, which means they typically appear earlier in a
fringe area's residential build-out, as a simple consequence of when each format's threshold
is crossed — not because of anything specific about the fringe location itself.** A fringe
area gaining households steadily will cross the lower thresholds required by trades, hardware,
and auto-service retail well before it crosses the higher threshold a full grocery anchor's
business model typically requires. Observing the earlier formats without the later one, at a
single point in time, is exactly what this ordinary sequencing process predicts for a market
still mid-build-out — it requires no separate explanation involving the location's quality or
a grocery operator's specific judgment about the site.

**This reasoning identifies a candidate explanation worth checking, not a conclusion that
applies automatically to every observed instance of the pattern.** A missing grocery anchor
can also genuinely reflect a location a grocery operator has evaluated and rejected — for
reasons unrelated to rooftop count, such as site access, competing locations already serving
the catchment, or a specific operator's unrelated strategic decision. The rooftop-sequencing
explanation and the rejected-site explanation produce the identical observable pattern from
a distance; distinguishing them requires looking at the specific site's actual and projected
rooftop trajectory, not just the current retail mix.

**The distinguishing question a land buyer should actually ask is trajectory, not
snapshot.** A fringe cluster whose surrounding residential rooftop count is measurably still
rising, and has not yet reached the threshold a grocery anchor's business model typically
requires, is consistent with the sequencing explanation. A fringe cluster whose rooftop count
has plateaued well above that threshold, with no grocery anchor present after a sustained
period, is more consistent with a specific site having been evaluated and passed over — a
materially different situation requiring a different, site-specific investigation into why.

## 3. How this framework applies

We recommend a land buyer apply this reasoning in two concrete steps before treating a
missing grocery anchor as either a positive or a negative signal. First, establish whether
the surrounding area's rooftop count is still actively growing, roughly stable, or in decline
— a trajectory question, answerable from ordinary demographic and building-permit data,
independent of any retail-specific dataset. Second, compare that trajectory against the
general rooftop threshold full-format grocery anchors typically require to operate — a
publicly discussed, order-of-magnitude figure in commercial retail-site-selection practice,
not a precise universal number, since it varies by grocery format and regional operating
model. A rising rooftop count still below that general threshold supports reading the
missing anchor as a sequencing fact — a market not yet there, not a market passed over. A
rooftop count already well above that threshold, sustained over time, with still no grocery
anchor present, is a different situation and warrants a specific, site-level investigation
rather than a sequencing-based explanation.

This is deliberately a screening heuristic, not a substitute for underwriting. It tells a
land buyer which of two very different explanations for an observed retail pattern is more
plausible before committing further diligence effort — it does not itself predict when, or
whether, a grocery anchor will actually arrive at any specific site.

## 4. What it changes

For a land buyer evaluating urban-fringe sites, the practical change is not discarding a
site simply because it currently lacks a grocery anchor, without first checking whether the
absence is explained by an ordinary, well-documented sequencing effect rather than by a
genuine locational weakness. Two fringe clusters showing the identical retail composition
today can be in very different real situations — one still climbing toward a rooftop
threshold it has not yet reached, the other having already passed that threshold with no
anchor arriving — and only the rooftop-trajectory check in §3 distinguishes them. Treating
every missing-anchor site identically, in either direction, discards real information a
buyer already has easy access to.

## 5. Where this could be wrong

**This paper makes no rooftop-threshold claim specific to any real site, and no forecast of
when or whether any specific location will receive a grocery anchor.** The general rooftop-
threshold logic described in §2 is well established in retail-location economics broadly; its
application to any particular fringe cluster requires that cluster's own real demographic
data, which this paper does not supply or claim to have analyzed.

**The rooftop-sequencing explanation is one plausible explanation for a missing anchor, not
the only one.** As stated directly in §2, a genuinely rejected site produces the same
observable pattern as a market still mid-sequence; this paper's contribution is naming the
distinguishing question, not eliminating the possibility that any specific site is a rejected
one rather than a mid-sequence one.

**Rooftop thresholds vary by grocery format and by region, and this paper does not supply a
precise, universal number.** Any land buyer applying this framework to a real decision needs
site-specific and format-specific data, not a general order-of-magnitude figure of the kind
described here.

## 6. Conclusion

A missing grocery anchor in an otherwise active urban-fringe retail cluster is commonly read
as evidence the market has been evaluated and passed over. General retail-location economics
suggests a real share of these cases are better explained by ordinary rooftop-threshold
sequencing — trades, hardware, and auto-service formats typically arrive earlier in a growing
market's build-out than a full grocery anchor, simply because their business models require
fewer nearby households to operate. The practical value of this framework is narrow but real:
it gives a land buyer a specific, checkable question — is the local rooftop count still
rising toward the grocery-anchor threshold, or has it plateaued above it with no anchor
arriving — that distinguishes a market not yet ready from a market genuinely passed over,
rather than treating every missing-anchor site the same way in either direction.

## 7. Claims and what would count against them

**Sequencing claim.** In a growing urban-fringe market, the observed order of retail-format
entry follows each format's rooftop threshold, with lower-threshold formats (trades, hardware,
auto-service) typically preceding higher-threshold formats (full-format grocery).

**Distinguishability claim.** A rising rooftop count still below the general grocery-anchor
threshold, versus a plateaued rooftop count already above it, are the two conditions that
distinguish a "not yet" fringe market from a "passed over" one, using data a land buyer can
obtain independent of a retail-specific survey.

| Test | What it checks | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Format-entry-order check | Whether observed retail-format entry timing correlates with each format's general rooftop threshold, across a sample of real fringe markets | Not tested in this paper — general economic reasoning stated, not verified against a specific dataset here |
| Trajectory-classification test | Whether the rising-vs-plateaued rooftop distinction actually predicts which missing-anchor sites later receive a grocery anchor | Not tested — no longitudinal tracking performed |

Both claims are falsified if a representative sample of urban-fringe markets shows no
correlation between rooftop trajectory and later grocery-anchor arrival, or if missing-anchor
sites turn out to be explained overwhelmingly by site-specific rejection rather than
sequencing, regardless of rooftop trajectory. Neither test has been run against real,
tracked site outcomes; this paper's contribution is the reasoning framework and the
distinguishing question, not a validated predictive model.
