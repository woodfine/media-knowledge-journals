Our thesis is that in development finance the order of the money matters more than the price
of it. The conventional sequence borrows first and earns later: a developer arranges a
construction loan sized against what the finished project is projected to cost, builds, and
then hopes the completed building earns enough to service what was borrowed to build it. The
sequence argued for here runs the other way. The first buildings are paid for with equity and
carry no construction debt at all. Only once they are finished, leased and producing rent does
the investment vehicle itself borrow — issuing its own mortgage-secured bonds against the
income those buildings are already earning — and that borrowing pays for the next phase.

The reason for inverting the order is not a general preference for low debt. It is a specific,
local obstacle. In the smaller regional markets these buildings are planned for, a commercial
lender will generally finance the ground-floor retail against a signed lease with a national
tenant, and two or three floors of offices above it — and, where none of the office space can be
pre-let at all, perhaps only one or two — and then stop, because the office space
higher up is genuinely hard to pre-lease in those markets, where a professional firm typically
wants a quarter of a floor rather than three floors, and where large institutional investors
are not present to stand behind the project. The practical consequence, repeated across those
markets, is a two- or three-storey building. The same amount of pre-leasing in a dense,
transit-served city supports a ten- to fifteen-storey one. Taking the lender out of the first
stage takes that ceiling out with it.

One boundary before we start, because at a glance two different questions can look like the
same one. This paper is about *whether and how well* a building gets financed. A separate
paper of ours, on the site that covers how these buildings are actually designed, is about
what physically limits a building's height on a given piece of land — which turns out to be
the geometry of its parking rather than anything financial. Those are not competing answers.
Geometry sets the ceiling a parcel can physically support. Financing decides whether, and how
well, a project reaches it.

## 1. The thesis

Sizing debt against income a building has already earned, rather than against a cost estimate
for a building that does not yet exist, is a fundamentally different — and, we argue, safer —
sequence than conventional construction financing. It is also, over a full development cycle,
a more productive one: the company's own financial model calculates that the same starting
capital ends up having built over forty per cent more square feet this way than through a
conventional joint venture. That is a calculation, not a result, and Section 6 is about
exactly how much weight it can bear.

Two things make this a real claim rather than a slogan.

The first is that the borrowing is done by the vehicle that owns the buildings, against those
buildings, rather than by a development company against its own balance sheet and reputation.
The debt is secured directly on the physical buildings and on nothing else. If a debenture
were ever to default, the transfer of the building behind it extinguishes that debt entirely;
the rest of the portfolio carries on; and investors cannot be called on for more money to
cover it, because the liability was never theirs personally in the first place.

The second is that the income has to exist before the debt does. That is the whole content of
the covenant described in Section 5, and it is a self-imposed constraint rather than a
lender's. A conventional lender is perfectly willing to lend against projections, because the
lender holds security and is paid for the risk. The constraint here does not come from a bank.
It is written into the vehicle's own governing agreement, which is a different thing entirely:
a bank's requirement can be renegotiated with the bank, while this one requires the investors
to vote.

## 2. The problem, in the reader's terms

Nothing in this section requires any knowledge of commercial property. It requires knowing how
a bank thinks about lending on a building, which is the same way a bank thinks about lending on
anything.

A lender's question is always some version of: what is paying this back, and what do I get if
it doesn't? For a finished, rented building the answers are easy. The rent pays it back; the
building is the security; the rent can be counted, because tenants have signed leases and
money is arriving. This is why a mortgage on a fully leased office building is one of the
more ordinary transactions in finance.

For a building that does not exist yet, both answers get harder. There is no rent, because
there are no tenants, because there is no building. So the lender asks for something that
stands in for the rent: leases signed *in advance* of construction, on space that does not yet
exist. That is **pre-leasing**, and it is the hinge of this whole argument. A developer who
arrives with signed pre-leases has converted a projection into a contract, and a lender will
lend against a contract.

Now put that mechanism into two different places.

In a large city with a rail station nearby, the ground-floor retail space can be pre-leased to
a substantial national tenant, and several floors of offices above it to two or three more
substantial tenants, before a shovel goes in the ground. With that much contracted income in
hand, and often a large institutional investor standing behind the project as well, a lender
will fund a ten- to fifteen-storey building.

In a town of forty thousand people with a large-format retail node at its edge and no rail at
all, the ground-floor retail can still be pre-leased — perhaps not to a national grocery
chain but to a local optometrist. The local law firm, engineering practice and accountancy
will sign too. They want a quarter of a floor each, and quite often they would rather see the
building finished before committing at all. So the developer arrives at the lender with the
ground floor let and very little above it, and the lender funds the ground floor and two or
three storeys, which is a perfectly rational decision on the lender's part and produces a
three-storey building.

It is worth being careful about the strength of that claim. It is not that no bank will
finance a taller building in a smaller market. It is that conventional financing becomes
materially harder, and the market's answer is systematically shorter buildings than the land
or the demand would support. That is the difficulty this sequence is built around. It removes
the lender from the stage at which the lender's question cannot be answered, and brings the
lender back in at the stage where it can be answered easily — after the building is standing
and the rent is arriving.

The everyday analogy is close to exact. A buyer who wants a rental property and has no cash
must persuade a lender to finance a purchase on the strength of rent nobody has yet paid. A
buyer who pays cash, lets the property, waits until the tenancy is established, and *then*
mortgages it, is asking the lender a much easier question, and will usually get a better
answer. The second buyer has not avoided debt. They have changed when the debt arrives, and
therefore what it is priced against.

## 3. What "narrow banking" means, and what it does not mean here

The name attached to this sequence is borrowed, and the borrowing should be explained rather
than assumed, because the original meaning is both real and narrower than the use made of it
here.

**Narrow banking** is a long-running idea in banking reform. An ordinary bank takes deposits
that can be withdrawn on demand and lends them out for years — borrowing short and lending
long, which is where most of the fragility in banking comes from, because a bank that must
repay today cannot get its money back from a twenty-year mortgage today. A narrow bank is an
institution that deliberately does not do this: its liabilities are demandable, and its assets
are correspondingly safe and short, so the two sides match. Proposals along these lines have
appeared after nearly every major financial crisis since the 1930s, and a substantial review of
the theoretical and empirical literature finds the evidence largely supportive of narrow-banking
reforms, chiefly because such a system contains the moral hazard that deposit insurance and
lender-of-last-resort support otherwise create [pennacchi-2012-narrow-banking].

What is borrowed here is the matching discipline, not the banking licence. These vehicles are
not banks, take no deposits, and make no loans. What they take from the idea is the refusal to
fund a long asset with a short, fragile claim. Applied to property development, the long asset
is a building with a fifty-year life, and the short fragile claim is a construction loan with a
four- or five-year term that must be refinanced at whatever rates and lending appetite happen
to exist on its maturity date. The sequence described here funds the long asset with permanent
equity — capital with no maturity date at all — and only issues term debt once there is
contracted income to match it against.

There is a second borrowed idea worth naming for the same reason. A conventional development
joint venture of the kind the group has used historically carried debt at roughly two and a
half times equity, with the cost of a construction loan running for four to five years before
the building earned anything. That is not a criticism of the joint-venture form, which built a
great many buildings. It is the specific cost this sequence is designed to remove: not the
interest rate, but the years of paying interest on a building that is not yet earning.

## 4. The equity phase: building with no debt at all

In the first phase, equity capital raised through the vehicle funds construction outright. No
pre-leasing is required before construction begins. No construction loan is drawn. The
completed buildings are, in the model's own term, **unlevered square feet** — finished,
rentable space carrying no debt, and therefore imposing no interest payment on the vehicle
during the months when the building is being leased up and is not yet full.

That is only possible because of the shape of the equity. In a conventional private real-estate
fund the capital has a deadline: the fund has a life, commonly seven to twelve years, at the
end of which assets must be sold and investors repaid. A manager with a deadline cannot
sensibly put money into an empty building for two years, because the clock is running. The
units in these vehicles carry no redemption date, no maturity and no compulsory exit event —
equity is held indefinitely, and an investor needing liquidity is expected to find a buyer
rather than to be redeemed by the issuer. Capital with no deadline can be put into a building
that will not earn for two years, because nothing forces the question to be answered sooner.

Two plain statements belong here rather than in a footnote. The first: there is a real cost to
this, and it falls on the early investors. Building without debt means the same equity buys
fewer square feet at the start than leveraged equity would — the model is explicit that this
sequence initially develops *fewer* unlevered square feet than a conventional joint venture on
the same money, and only overtakes it later in the cycle. An investor whose horizon is the
first phase rather than the full cycle is, on the model's own arithmetic, worse off. The
second: the perpetual structure that makes this possible is also the structure that makes
these units illiquid, and the units of the one established vehicle cannot be transferred at
all today, because that partnership is subject to an active cease-trade order issued by the
British Columbia Securities Commission, arising from delayed continuous-disclosure filings
rather than any finding of fraud or misconduct. A separate paper of ours deals with what
transferability does and does not give a holder; it is named here so that a reader does not
leave this section believing perpetual equity is costless.

## 5. The debt phase: how the borrowing is sized once it exists

Once income from completed, leased buildings has stabilised, the vehicle is planned to issue
**First Secured Mortgage Debentures** — its own bonds, secured in first position directly
against the physical buildings, to fund the next construction phase rather than to refinance
what is already built.

Several features of that borrowing are worth a reader's attention, and each carries its
qualification in the same breath.

**The coverage test.** New debentures may not be issued if issuing them would push the
vehicle's **interest coverage ratio** below 1.20 times. Interest coverage is a simple idea:
net operating income divided by the annual interest the vehicle owes. At 1.20 times, the
buildings are earning one dollar and twenty cents of operating income for every dollar of
interest due. The test is applied across the vehicle as a whole rather than property by
property. Three honest qualifications sit on it. It is a floor on *new borrowing*, not a
continuously monitored trigger — it is tested at the moment of issuance, against an income
figure that is by then historical, and nothing about it re-tests the vehicle if income later
falls. It excludes principal repayment, which makes it a more lenient measure than the debt
service coverage ratio a commercial lender would normally impose. And it can be waived: the
vehicle may exceed the floor with a special resolution of unit holders, which requires
seventy-five per cent support. It is a high bar, deliberately, and it is not an absolute
unconditional ceiling, and a reader should hold both of those facts at once.

**The ceiling on quantum.** The maximum debt under the model is one hundred per cent of
development costs for new construction. Debentures fund the building of additional buildings;
they do not fund the refinancing of buildings already owned, which is the mechanism by which
leverage in conventional portfolios tends to ratchet upward without anything new being built.
During the active construction phase, the model expects portfolio debt to reach no more than
about sixty-three per cent of asset value.

**Interest cannot be hidden.** Each vehicle's governing agreement prohibits **capitalised
interest** — the practice of adding interest owed during construction to the loan's principal
rather than paying it as it accrues. Capitalising interest is common and legal and it has one
consistent effect: debt grows during construction without the cost showing up in current
income. Prohibiting it means the cost of the borrowing appears in the period it is incurred.

**Debt comes down on its own.** In the stabilised phase, ten per cent of distributable income
is intended to be retained and applied to redeeming debentures — with the intent that leverage
falls toward roughly half of asset value over the hold period rather than merely drifting
downward. The mechanism is the vehicle's own cash flow, not a favourable refinancing market or
an asset sale. The distribution rule this comes from is worth stating precisely, because it is
not discretionary generosity: where a distribution is declared, at least ninety per cent of
the fiscal year's distributable income is paid to limited partners within ninety days of year
end, and until cumulative distributions equal the whole of what limited partners paid for
their units, the retained ten per cent is held for partnership purposes; after that point it
goes to redeeming debentures until they are fully redeemed.

**Existing investors get first refusal on the debt.** When the vehicle issues debentures,
existing equity holders have a right of first refusal — the opportunity to buy into that debt
before it is offered to outside purchasers. An investor who wants exposure to both halves of
the same buildings' capital structure can have it.

**The debentures are designed to qualify for listing, not to trade privately.** The intended
design meets the applicable exchange's own listing standards, with the intent to list the
debentures on it, while the equity units themselves remain unlisted. Meeting an exchange's
standards and a security actually trading on it are two different steps, and it is the first
of those two the design targets today — no debenture has yet been issued, so no listing has
yet occurred either. In the Mexican vehicle's planned design the same distinction is explicit:
the debt instrument is intended to trade on that country's exchanges once issued, while the
equity certificates stay off any trading board. A reader should read "exchange-qualified"
here as the accurate present-tense description — the design is built to meet listing
standards — and "exchange-traded" as the intended outcome once an actual issuance occurs, not
as a current fact.

## 6. What the model claims, and what it does not prove

The capital-efficiency claim deserves its own section because it is the single number most
likely to be quoted out of this paper, and it needs its qualifications attached to it rather
than trailing behind.

The claim is this: by the end of a full development cycle, and starting from exactly the same
initial capital, this sequence develops over forty per cent more square feet than a
conventional legacy joint venture. The mechanism is compounding rather than cleverness. Because
the developer's profit is taken as equity rather than drawn out as cash, and because a portion
of income is retained rather than fully distributed, capital accumulates inside the vehicle and
is available to build again — each cycle starting from a slightly larger base, with debt raised
against income that already exists rather than against the next projection.

Now the qualifications, and they are substantial.

This is a model output. It is a forward-looking calculation from a set of stated assumptions
about construction cost, lease-up timing, rent levels, interest rates and holding periods. It
is not a measurement of anything that has happened. No Woodfine Building has been constructed.
No First Secured Mortgage Debenture has been issued. No completed portfolio exists against
which either the equity phase or the leverage ceiling has been tested. The comparison against a
conventional joint venture is a comparison against a modelled alternative, not against a real
competing project that was run in parallel.

The model also loses in the early years before it wins in the later ones, and the crossover
point is itself a model assumption. Anyone assessing this should ask when the crossover is
projected to occur and what happens to the answer if lease-up takes longer than assumed —
because an unlevered building that stays empty is a pure cost with no offsetting interest
saving to show for it.

Our position is that a model claim of this kind should be published with its parameters
attached, so that a reader can disagree with the assumptions rather than only with the
conclusion. The forecast methodology behind these vehicles is documented publicly in outline:
distributions are reconciled to distributable income rather than to accounting net income,
specifically excluding the non-cash revaluation gains that cannot actually be distributed, and
the development-phase debenture is modelled at amortised cost so that the effective borrowing
rate the forecast shows is higher than the coupon on the instrument's face. That is the right
direction. It is not yet the same as publishing the parameter set behind the forty per cent
figure, and until that is published the figure should be read as a claim we are making rather
than one a reader can check.

## What this changes for the reader

The first change is that "capital-efficient" stops being an adjective and becomes an
arithmetic claim with a mechanism attached. An investor can ask three specific questions of
this structure, or of any other that claims something similar. Is there construction debt on
the first buildings? What is the borrowing sized against — a cost projection, or income already
contracted? And what happens to the covenant if income falls after the debt is raised? Those
three answers tell a reader more than any amount of language about prudence.

The second is a way of reading the pre-leasing question that generalises well beyond this
company. When a reader is told that a market cannot support a taller or better building, it is
worth asking whether that is a fact about the market's demand or a fact about how the market is
financed. Those get conflated constantly, and they have different remedies. Demand cannot be
engineered. A financing sequence can be changed.

The third is a plain statement of where the risk went. It did not disappear. In the
conventional sequence, the risk of a building not leasing sits substantially with a lender, who
is paid to hold it and who has security and remedies. In this sequence it sits with the equity
— which is to say with the investors — during the whole of the construction and lease-up
period. That is the trade. A reader who prefers the conventional arrangement is not making a
mistake; they are choosing to have a professional lender in the deal, priced accordingly.

The trade-offs belong together in one place. Equity-funded construction means the earliest
investors fund the least efficient phase of the cycle. The coverage covenant is waivable by a
supermajority and is tested only when new debt is issued, so it constrains borrowing rather
than guaranteeing solvency. The capital-efficiency claim is modelled and unverified. And the
whole sequence depends on being able to raise substantial equity before any building exists,
which is a real commercial constraint and not a technical one: at the date of this paper, one
vehicle of four is established, and its units are subject to an active cease-trade order.

## An open invitation

The central question in this paper is an empirical one that we cannot settle from inside the
company.

To real-estate finance academics: the honest open question is whether income-sequenced,
equity-first development genuinely outperforms conventional leveraged development across a
full market cycle *including a downturn*. Our model says it does over a cycle it assumes. The
interesting test is the cycle it does not assume: a period where rents fall while an unlevered
building is in lease-up, or where the coverage covenant blocks the next phase precisely when
land is cheapest. Leverage is not only a cost; in a rising market it is the mechanism by which
a developer captures more of the rise. A structure that forgoes it in the first phase forgoes
that too. We would rather see that tested against this model's published parameters than
assume it either way.

To the banking-reform literature: we have borrowed the narrow-banking framing because the
matching discipline genuinely is what we are doing, and we are aware that the original idea is
about deposit-taking institutions and systemic fragility, not about property development. If
the analogy breaks somewhere important — if there is a reason the matching argument works for a
bank's balance sheet and does not carry across to a development vehicle's — that is the kind of
correction that would change how we describe this rather than merely how we defend it.

To credit analysts and rating professionals: the interest coverage covenant in this structure
is deliberately more lenient than the debt service coverage ratio a commercial lender would
impose, it excludes principal repayment, it is tested at issuance rather than continuously,
and it is waivable by vote. We think that combination is defensible for a vehicle with no
external construction debt and no refinancing wall. We would like to be told if it is not,
and specifically whether a covenant tested only at issuance is worth anything at all in a
downturn.

To anyone with experience of a development programme that ran this sequence: the forty per
cent figure is a calculation, and the most useful thing anyone could hand us is a real case —
a programme that built unlevered and then borrowed against completed income, over a full cycle,
with what actually happened to the compounding. We have not found one at this scale. If it
exists, it is worth more to this argument than any refinement of our own model.

## Conclusion

The sequence is the claim: equity first, with no construction debt on the earliest buildings,
then debt issued by the vehicle itself and sized against income those buildings are already
earning. It answers a specific, local problem — that in smaller regional markets a lender's
perfectly rational question about pre-leased income produces buildings two or three storeys
tall — by removing the lender from the stage where that question cannot be answered. The
capital-efficiency result that follows, over forty per cent more built space from the same
initial capital by the end of a development cycle, is a model output and should be read as one:
nothing has been built, no debenture has been issued, and the model wins late after losing
early. What a reader can hold us to is not the figure but the sequence, and the covenant, and
the published parameters when they arrive.

holders — are terms of the established Canada vehicle's own governing agreement. They are
commitments rather than measurements, and they are specific to that vehicle; the three planned
vehicles will be governed by separate instruments. A reader relying on any of them should read
the agreement.

The construction-lending description — that a lender in these markets will typically finance
ground-floor retail pre-leased to a national tenant plus two or three floors above it, against
a ten- to fifteen-storey building on comparable pre-leasing in a dense transit-served market —
is our own observation from prior development work in these markets rather than a study. We
have not attempted to quantify it, and it is the kind of claim that varies by lender, by
country and by year.

The capital-efficiency figure — over forty per cent more square feet by the end of a full
development cycle on the same initial capital — is an output of the company's own financial
model, calculated from stated assumptions about cost, timing, rents and rates. It is not an
observed result, and it does not describe a completed project, because none exists. The same
model shows this sequence developing fewer square feet than the conventional comparison in the

sixty-three per cent during construction and the intended stabilisation near half of asset

been published; until it is, a reader cannot reconstruct the forty per cent figure, and should
treat it accordingly.

The debentures are designed to meet the applicable exchange's listing standards, with the
intent to list them once issued; the equity units are not intended to be listed. No debenture

figures, or verified this paper's description of the governing agreement.
