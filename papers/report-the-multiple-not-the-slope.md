Start with two hypothetical investments, because the arithmetic makes the argument better than
the argument does. Both run for ten years. The first hands back half of your original capital
at the end of year four, nothing for the next six years, and then a final payment that brings
the total you have received to twice what you put in. The second pays nothing at all until year
ten and then pays out — also twice what you put in. Over the ten years you received exactly the
same amount of money from both. Ask for each one's internal rate of return, the figure that
headlines almost every private-market presentation, and the first is materially higher than the
second.

Now change the second arrangement so that it still pays nothing until year ten, but then pays
out roughly half again as much: not twice your capital, but close to three times it. Its
internal rate of return is now identical to the first arrangement's. Two investments, the same
headline figure, and one of them hands you half again as much money.

Our thesis is that the figure which should lead is the multiple of capital actually returned —
what came back, divided by what went in — rather than a rate of return. A rate measures speed.
An investor is paid in size. Both are legitimate things to measure and there are real cases
where the speed matters, which we set out below. But only one of them answers the question the
holder of a unit is actually asking, and it is not the one the industry puts in the headline.

## 1. The thesis

A rate of return is a measurement of pace. The multiple of capital returned is a measurement of
outcome. When they disagree — and they disagree constantly, because they are computed from
different information — the outcome is the figure an investor should be shown first.

Our position is that these vehicles should report what came back and what the buildings were
independently appraised at, in audited statements, rather than headline a rate of return at all.
That is a position about presentation, and it costs us something: a rate of return is the figure
the market is used to, and choosing not to lead with it makes a document harder to compare
against the documents beside it. We think that is the right cost to pay, for a reason that has
nothing to do with modesty. A perpetual vehicle has no terminal exit date — there is no
compulsory liquidation event at which everything is sold and a final rate is struck — so a
since-inception rate of return for a structure like this one would be a number without a natural
end point, recomputed forever from an ever-lengthening early history. Section 3 is about why
that specific property makes such a figure unusually misleading.

## 2. The problem, in the reader's terms

Two ideas are needed, and both are ordinary once separated.

A **multiple of capital** is a ratio of totals. You put in a sum; over some period you receive
distributions; the multiple is everything you received divided by what you put in. It ignores
timing entirely. It is the answer to "how much did I get back." Two times your capital is two
times your capital whether it arrived in year three or year thirty.

A **rate of return** is a measure of growth per unit of time, and there are several of them.
The plainest is a compound growth rate: begin with a value, end with a value, and work out the
steady annual rate that would connect the two. It is blind to everything that happened in
between. The **internal rate of return** is more sophisticated and less intuitive: it is the
single annual rate at which every payment in and out, discounted back to the start, exactly
cancels. Because each payment is weighted by when it happened, money returned early counts for
a great deal, and money returned late counts for much less. That is not a defect — it is
precisely what the measure is built to capture — but it means an internal rate of return is
principally a statement about *timing*, dressed in the same clothes as a statement about
*amount*.

The everyday version of this confusion is familiar to anyone who has held a savings account and
a mortgage at the same time. Two accounts quoted at the same annual rate can leave very
different sums in them at the end, depending on when money went in and came out, how often
interest was credited, and over how long. The quoted rate is true and it is not the answer to
"how much will I have." A borrower who has ever compared two loans with the same headline rate
and found very different total costs has already met this problem; the private-investment
version is the same problem with the sign reversed and the stakes larger.

There is one more feature worth naming, because it is the part that makes a rate of return
genuinely hard to read rather than merely partial. A rate is a *derivative* — a measurement of
change per unit of time, the slope of a line rather than a point on it. A multiple is a plain
ratio. Asking a reader to compare two investments by their slopes, without showing them where
either line ended up, is asking a lot; and it is what a presentation does when its front page
carries a rate and its appendix carries the distributions.

## 3. What an internal rate of return actually rewards

Three properties of the measure matter here, and all three are documented in the research
literature rather than being complaints of ours.

**It rewards early cash above total cash.** This follows directly from the discounting: a
payment in year four is worth more to the calculation than the same payment in year ten. An
arrangement that returns capital early and then does very little will often post a better
internal rate of return than one that returns much more, later.

**Once it is high, it can stay high almost mechanically.** A firm whose earliest investments did
well sets its since-inception internal rate of return at a level that the arithmetic of the
measure then tends to preserve, more or less indefinitely, as long as no disaster intervenes.
The early years do most of the work and later years struggle to move the figure in either
direction. The consequence is that a since-inception rate can describe a firm's first decade
long after that decade has stopped being relevant [phalippou-2020-inconvenient-fact].

**It is easier to flatter on some investments than on others.** The same research makes a
further point that is easy to miss and has real distributional consequences: the features that
allow an internal rate of return to be managed upward are more readily available in some kinds
of deal and some markets than in others. Whole categories of genuinely sound investment can
therefore look permanently mediocre by comparison, for reasons that are properties of the
measurement rather than of the investments [phalippou-2020-inconvenient-fact].

Alongside those three, there is an honest case *for* the measure, and this paper would be
dishonest without it. Capital returned early is genuinely worth more than capital returned
late, because it can be put to work again. An investor who gets half their money back in year
four and reinvests it successfully may well end up better off than one who waited. That is the
real reason internal rate of return exists and it is not a trick. The objection is not that the
measure is meaningless; it is that it answers a question about *timing* and is routinely
presented as the answer to a question about *total return* — and that when the two answers
conflict, a reader is given the one that flatters.

## 4. The illustration, worked through

Take the three arrangements from the opening and set them out properly. All are hypothetical
illustrations of arithmetic. They are not projections, not offerings, and not descriptions of
anything Woodfine has or intends to produce.

**Arrangement A.** Runs ten years. Returns half of the original capital at the end of year
four. Nothing further until year ten, when a final payment brings the total received to twice
the original capital. Multiple of capital returned: two times.

**Arrangement B.** Runs ten years. Returns nothing until year ten, then pays out. Total
received: twice the original capital. Multiple of capital returned: two times.

**Arrangement C.** Runs ten years. Returns nothing until year ten, then pays out. Total
received: close to three times the original capital. Multiple of capital returned: nearly three
times.

Now the two comparisons that matter.

*A against B.* Identical multiples — both return twice the capital — and A posts the higher
internal rate of return, because half the capital came back six years earlier. A reader shown
only the rates would conclude A was the better arrangement. A reader shown only the multiples
would conclude they were the same. The truth is that they returned the same money and A returned
some of it sooner, which is an advantage of a specific and limited kind: it is worth exactly what
the investor could earn on the returned half over those six years, and no more. The rate does
not tell you that; it asserts a general superiority.

*A against C.* Here the measures fail in the more serious direction. Their internal rates of
return are the same. Their multiples are not remotely the same: C returns roughly half again as
much money in total. A reader comparing the two on the headline figure would see no difference
between an arrangement that doubles their capital and one that nearly triples it. There is no
reading of "rate of return" under which that is an acceptable presentation of the difference,
and it is not an edge case constructed to embarrass the measure — it is what happens whenever
timing and total move in opposite directions, which is most of the time.

The conclusion we draw from that pair is narrow and, we think, hard to argue with. A rate of
return cannot distinguish A from C, and a multiple can. Whatever else is reported, the multiple
has to be there.

Two things belong in this discussion rather than in a separate confession at the end, because
they are part of the argument rather than a qualification of it.

The first is that the strongest published evidence about private markets cuts against the
premise of a private structure — ours included. Research on private-market performance finds
that, net of all fees, large public pension investors have received in the region of one and a
half times their money from private-equity funds, that the largest firms and several
independent datasets land in the same narrow band on that basis, and that this is broadly what
public equity markets returned over the same period. It further finds that the
performance-related payments those funds collected across a single decade of fund vintages ran
to hundreds of billions of dollars regardless [phalippou-2020-inconvenient-fact]. The same
author's earlier work explains how the industry's own aggregate figures can be far above
academic estimates of the same funds without anybody lying, purely as a consequence of the
measurement conventions each side uses [phalippou-2011-confusing-evidence]. Note what those
findings are expressed in: multiples. That is not incidental. The finding is legible *because*
it is a multiple.

The second is what follows for us specifically. If the honest baseline expectation for a
private structure is roughly what a public index returns, then a private structure has to
justify itself on something other than an assertion of higher returns — on the shape of the
fee, on what the asset actually is, on the absence of a forced-sale date, on what the investor
can verify. Those are the arguments our other papers make. And any figure we publish about the
future is a projection: nothing has been built, no debenture has been issued, and no performance
figure of any kind has been reported for any of these vehicles. This paper quotes no projected return figure for any
Woodfine vehicle, and a reader should treat the absence as deliberate rather than as an
omission.

## 5. What is actually intended to be reported

It is easier to say what should not lead a report than to say what should, so here is the
positive commitment, with its limits.

What each vehicle is planned to file, as a reporting issuer, is a package rather than a
headline: audited annual financial statements prepared under International Financial Reporting
Standards with an independent auditor's report attached, management's discussion and analysis of
the year including the assumptions behind any forward-looking statement, interim statements
through the year, and periodic independent appraisals of the buildings themselves [ni-51-102].
All of it goes on the public filing record, where anyone can read it without asking
[sedar-plus]. An **appraisal**, in this context, is a professional valuation of a specific
building by a qualified valuer — an outside estimate of what the asset is worth, which for a
building that is not for sale is the only honest answer available.

Two features of that package are worth pointing out because they are where a rate-of-return
headline would otherwise hide something.

Distributions are reconciled to **distributable income** rather than to accounting net income.
Under international accounting standards, a property portfolio's reported profit includes
revaluation gains — the increase in what the buildings are said to be worth — which are real
accounting income and are not cash. They cannot be distributed, because nobody has paid them to
anybody. Reconciling to distributable income means the figure a holder reads as available to be
paid out excludes those non-cash gains. This is the same discipline as the fee base described in
another of our papers, applied to the other side of the ledger.

And the distribution rule itself is a stated minimum rather than a discretion. Where a
distribution is declared, at least ninety per cent of the fiscal year's distributable income is
paid to limited partners within ninety days of the year end; until cumulative distributions
equal the whole of what limited partners paid for their units, the remaining tenth is retained,
and after that point it is applied to redeeming the vehicle's debentures. That rule is what
makes a multiple of capital returned a meaningful figure to track here at all: there is a
defined relationship between what the buildings earn and what reaches a holder, rather than a
manager's unconstrained judgement about when to distribute.

Now the blunt part. None of this has happened. No annual or interim filing has been made under
this reporting model, no appraisal series exists, no distribution history exists, and therefore
no multiple of capital returned exists to report. The one established vehicle is subject to an
active cease-trade order issued by the British Columbia Securities Commission, arising from
delayed continuous-disclosure filings rather than any finding of fraud or misconduct, and must
bring its filings current and obtain the Commission's revocation before any future offering of
units can close. A paper arguing for better performance reporting, written by a company whose
filings went late, should say that in the section about reporting rather than in the small print
— so it is here.

## What this changes for the reader

The first change is a question that works on any investment presentation, from any sponsor, in
any asset class: *what multiple of my capital came back, and over what period?* If the answer
is a rate of return, the question has not been answered. If the answer is "we report internal
rate of return," a reasonable follow-up is to ask for total distributions and total
contributions, which are two numbers the sponsor certainly has.

The second is a specific trap worth recognising. Two investments with the same headline rate of
return can differ enormously in what they returned; and an investment with a lower rate can
return more money. A reader who now flinches at a rate-of-return headline standing alone has
taken the whole of this paper's practical content.

The third is about the word "since inception." A since-inception rate of return for a
long-running manager is doing something quite different from what it appears to: it is largely a
statement about the manager's earliest years, preserved by the arithmetic of the measure. If it
is quoted without the multiple beside it, and without the period broken out, it should be read
as marketing rather than as measurement.

The trade-offs are real and they run against us as well as for us. A multiple ignores time
entirely, and time genuinely matters: two times your money over ten years and two times your
money over thirty are not the same investment, and anyone using only multiples will eventually
be misled in that direction. Our answer is that a multiple plus the period plus audited
statements plus the appraisals is a more honest package than a rate standing alone — not that a
multiple alone is sufficient. And there is a real cost to reporting in a way the rest of the
market does not: a reader trying to compare this structure against a conventional fund will have
to do arithmetic the fund's own presentation would have done for them. We think that is
preferable to doing the arithmetic in a way that flatters us, but it is a cost, and it falls on
the reader rather than on us.

## An open invitation

The questions here belong to the people who study how performance gets reported, and the most
useful of them are questions we cannot answer from inside a company that has not yet reported
anything.

To researchers on private-market performance measurement: the open question is what would
actually shift standard industry practice toward multiple-based headline reporting. The
analytical case has been made repeatedly and well for two decades, and practice has barely
moved, which suggests the obstacle is not analytical. Whether it is a coordination problem —
nobody can report differently while everyone else reports the flattering figure — or a
regulatory gap, or simply that the audience prefers a single number, is an empirical question
with a practical answer attached. We are choosing to report differently and we expect it to cost
us comparability. We would like to know whether anyone has measured that cost.

To researchers on perpetual and open-ended vehicle reporting: the measurement problem this paper
raises is sharper for a vehicle with no terminal date than for a closed-end fund, and we have
not solved it. A closed-end fund eventually liquidates and a final multiple exists. A perpetual
vehicle never does, so its multiple of capital returned is always an interim figure that will
keep rising for as long as the thing exists — which raises an obvious risk of a number that
looks better every year for no reason other than the passage of time. What the right convention
is for reporting outcome on an instrument with no end date is genuinely unresolved, and it is
the most important open question in this paper.

To auditors and accounting-standards specialists: the reconciliation between accounting profit
that includes unrealised revaluation and cash that can actually be distributed is where the
honest number lives, and it is presented in different places and different ways across the
industry. A standard presentation of that reconciliation, prominent rather than in a note, would
do more for investor comprehension than any amount of change to the headline metric.

## Conclusion

An internal rate of return tells an investor how quickly capital came back. It cannot tell them
how much came back, and the two are routinely different — two arrangements with the same rate
can differ by half again in what they actually pay out. Our position is that the multiple of
capital returned should lead, alongside audited statements and independent appraisals of the
buildings, with the period stated plainly and the timing available to anyone who wants it. The
published evidence that private-market returns have historically tracked public markets on a
multiple basis is part of the argument rather than an embarrassment to it: a structure that
cannot honestly claim a higher return has to justify itself on what it is, and reporting in a
way a reader can check is part of that. Nothing has been reported here yet. When it is, the
figure to ask for is the one that says how much came back.

## Data and reproducibility

The three arrangements in section 4 are arithmetic, not data. They are hypothetical: an
investment returning half of contributed capital in year four and the balance at year ten to a
total of twice contributed capital; one returning twice contributed capital at year ten alone;
and one returning close to three times contributed capital at year ten alone. Any reader with a
spreadsheet can reproduce both comparisons — that the first two share a multiple while the first
posts the higher internal rate of return, and that the first and third share an internal rate of
return while the third returns substantially more — in a few minutes, and we would encourage it,
because the point of the illustration is that it is checkable rather than asserted. We have
deliberately stated these in multiples and timing rather than in rates, so that no figure in this
paper can be mistaken for a projection of investment performance.

The findings attributed to published research — that private-market funds have returned in the
region of one and a half times invested capital net of fees to large investors, that this is
broadly comparable to public equity returns over the same period, that performance fees
collected over one decade of fund vintages ran into hundreds of billions of dollars, and that
since-inception internal rates of return are sticky and unevenly gameable — are the cited
author's, published and available at the sources listed above. A reader who wants to weigh those
findings should read the papers rather than our summary of them; they are contested in the
industry, and the rebuttals from large firms and the industry association are discussed in the
2020 paper itself.

The reporting commitments — the audited annual and interim filing package on an IFRS basis, the
reconciliation of distributions to distributable income excluding non-cash revaluation gains,
the at-least-ninety-per-cent distribution rule and its ninety-day timing, and the independent
appraisals — are terms of a governing agreement and of a documented forecast methodology for the
established Canada vehicle. They are commitments, not a record. Nothing in this paper reports an
outcome, because no outcome exists yet: there is no distribution history, no appraisal series,
and no multiple of capital returned for any vehicle. No independent party has reviewed this
paper's arithmetic, its account of the cited research, or its description of the intended
reporting package.
